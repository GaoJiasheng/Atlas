<Lang en>

<Term id="steam-engine">A steam engine</Term> uses steam to do mechanical work. On the stage, follow the boiler to the <Term id="cylinder">cylinder</Term>, the chamber containing the moving <Term id="piston">piston</Term>. Above it, the beam carries the motion towards the large wheel. This study takes the preserved Lap engine of <Num s="S2">1788</Num> as its reference. It is a reconstructed explanation of a mechanism, rather than a copy of every surviving detail. [S2, S7] <FlyTo preset="hero">Look over the whole engine</FlyTo>

Steam engines were pumping mine water before Watt’s improvements. The separate comparison station shows an earlier atmospheric arrangement. Watt’s working model, condenser patent and commercial machines belong to different stages; the machine on this stage combines later developments. A date attached to one improvement is not the invention date of the complete machine. [S11, S12, S13]

Follow three connected processes: water changes state, pressure acts on the piston, and linked parts transmit motion. The topic <TopicLink slug="states-of-matter" ch="gases">States of Matter: gases</TopicLink> introduces the gaseous state. Here, the coloured dots show routes through pipes. They are not individual molecules, measured flow rates or a temperature scale. [S1, S9]

Use the bottom chapter rail to choose a still frame, then rotate the model to inspect the connections. SIM marks a calculated teaching example. Pressure readings do not drive the animation: the mechanism follows a chosen phase, while its rigid links stay connected. Gaps are enlarged so that moving parts can be seen. Industrial history has a separate reading topic covering people, materials and wider changes. [S1]

Hulse reports a flywheel diameter of <Num s="S5">16 ft</Num>, about <Num s="S5">4.88 m</Num>. The conversions follow NIST length definitions; they add no historical measurement precision. [S16]
