<Lang en>

A grasshopper has no lungs. Along each side of its body is a row of small holes, the <Term id="spiracle">spiracles</Term>: <Num s="S7">ten</Num> pairs, <Num s="S7">two</Num> on the thorax and <Num s="S7">eight</Num> on the abdomen. Each can open and close. <FlyTo preset="side">Find the spiracles</FlyTo>

From each spiracle an air tube, a <Term id="trachea">trachea</Term>, runs inside. The tubes join into trunks along the body and branch again and again until the finest reach every cell, so oxygen arrives through air-filled tubes. Large thin-walled air sacs act as bellows. When muscles squeeze the abdomen the sacs empty; when it relaxes they fill. In a related locust resting at about <Num s="S25">25 °C</Num> the abdomen pumps about <Num s="S25">43–52</Num> times a minute. Air usually goes in at the front spiracles and out at the back ones, so it moves through the body in one direction. <FlyTo preset="systems">See the air tubes</FlyTo>

The blood works differently from ours too. A grasshopper has an <Term id="open-circulation">open circulation</Term>: there is only one main vessel, the <Term id="dorsal-vessel">dorsal vessel</Term> along the back. Its rear part, the heart, draws blood in through valved openings in its sides and pumps it forwards; at about <Num s="S25">25 °C</Num> it beats about <Num s="S25">76–80</Num> times a minute in a resting locust. The blood pours out in the head and seeps back past the organs. This blood, the <Term id="haemolymph">haemolymph</Term>, is colourless or pale yellow. It carries food, wastes, hormones and blood cells, but hardly any oxygen.

Cut across the thorax, the order from top to bottom is the reverse of ours: the heart lies along the back, the gut in the middle and the nerve cord along the belly.

<More title={{ en: "Air tubes and body size", zh: "气管和体型" }}>

Air moves along the finest tubes mainly by diffusion, which is fast over short distances but slow over long ones. Many biologists think this is one reason insects are small: a bigger insect needs longer tubes and more space for them. In darkling beetles the volume of the air tubes grows faster than body mass, as mass to the power <Num s="S28">1.29</Num>, so in the largest beetles the tubes may take up much of the space in the legs. The idea is still debated, and other limits have been proposed.

</More>

<More title={{ en: "Warmth and breathing in Singapore", zh: "温度、呼吸和新加坡" }}>

Insects do not keep a constant body temperature; their bodies warm and cool with their surroundings, and their chemistry runs faster when warm. In one North American grasshopper, the breathing rate rose from about <Num s="S8">6</Num> a minute at about <Num s="S8">9 °C</Num> to about <Num s="S8">27</Num> a minute at about <Num s="S8">27 °C</Num>.

Singapore stays warm all year, with monthly mean temperatures between about <Num s="S31">27 and 29 °C</Num>, close to the warm end of that range, and there is no cold season.

<Lang zh>

蚱蜢没有肺。它身体两侧各有一排小孔，叫<Term id="spiracle">气门</Term>，共 <Num s="S7">十</Num>对：胸部 <Num s="S7">两</Num>对，腹部 <Num s="S7">八</Num>对。每个气门都能开能关。<FlyTo preset="side">找一找气门</FlyTo>

每个气门都连着一根通进体内的空气管，叫<Term id="trachea">气管</Term>。气管在体内汇成纵干，又一再分支，最细的分支通到每个细胞旁边，所以氧气是经过充满空气的管子送到的。几个大而薄的气囊像风箱一样工作：肌肉挤压腹部，气囊就排空；腹部放松，气囊又充满。近缘的蝗虫在约 <Num s="S25">25℃</Num> 静息时，腹部每分钟泵气约 <Num s="S25">43–52</Num> 次。空气通常从前面的气门进、从后面的气门出，在体内单向流动。<FlyTo preset="systems">看看气管</FlyTo>

蚱蜢的血液循环也和我们不一样。它是<Term id="open-circulation">开放式循环</Term>：全身只有一条主要的血管，就是沿着背部的<Term id="dorsal-vessel">背血管</Term>。背血管后段是心脏，血液从两侧带瓣的开口流进来，再被向前泵；约 <Num s="S25">25℃</Num> 时，静息的蝗虫心跳约每分钟 <Num s="S25">76–80</Num> 次。血液在头部涌出，再绕过各个器官慢慢流回。这种血液叫<Term id="haemolymph">血淋巴</Term>，无色或淡黄色，运送养分、废物、激素和血细胞，却几乎不运送氧气。

横着切开胸部，从上到下的顺序和我们正好相反：心脏在背上，消化道在中间，神经索沿着腹面。

<More title={{ en: "Air tubes and body size", zh: "气管和体型" }}>

空气在最细的气管里主要靠扩散移动：距离短时很快，距离长时就很慢。许多生物学家认为，这是昆虫长不大的原因之一：昆虫越大，需要的气管越长，占的空间也越多。拟步甲的气管体积比体重增加得快，与体重的 <Num s="S28">1.29</Num> 次方成正比，所以最大的甲虫，足里的空间可能大半被气管占去。这个说法还有争议，也有人提出其他的限制。

</More>
