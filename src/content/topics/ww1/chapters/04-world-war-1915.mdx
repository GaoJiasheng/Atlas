The war soon reached far beyond Europe. Japan, Britain's ally, declared war on Germany on 23 August 1914. Its troops landed at Longkou in neutral China and, with about <Num s="S82">1,500</Num> British soldiers, took the German base at Tsingtao (Qingdao) on 7 November. Japan also took Germany's north Pacific islands; New Zealand, Australia, Britain, France and South Africa took Samoa, New Guinea and Germany's African colonies, though in East Africa a German force fought on until <Num s="S86">25 November 1918</Num>.

The German cruiser Emden sank a Russian cruiser and a French destroyer at Penang on 28 October 1914. In Singapore on 15 February 1915 about half of the <Num s="S94">818</Num> men of the Indian 5th Light Infantry mutinied, after rumours that they would be sent to fight the Muslim Ottoman Empire. They killed <Num s="S93">49</Num> people, most of them British; <Num s="S94">41</Num> to <Num s="S93">47</Num> were then executed, many in public. <FlyTo preset="singapore-penang">Singapore and Penang</FlyTo>

On 25 April Entente troops landed at Gallipoli to open the Dardanelles. By January 1916 they had left; about <Num s="S97">49,000</Num> to <Num s="S98">58,000</Num> Entente and about <Num s="S97,S98">87,000</Num> Ottoman soldiers had died. In the east, Germany and Austria-Hungary broke through at Gorlice on 2 May and took all of Poland; the retreating Russian army expelled about <Num s="S104">200,000</Num> Jews from Kovno and Kurland in May alone. Italy joined the Entente on 23 May. In October Germany, Austria-Hungary and Bulgaria overran Serbia, whose army retreated over the Albanian mountains in winter.

From 24 April 1915 the Ottoman government carried out the <Term id="genocide">genocide</Term> of its Armenian subjects. Men were killed; women, children and the old were marched into the Syrian desert, where many starved or were massacred. Scholars estimate <Num s="S108">664,000</Num> to <Num s="S107">1.5 million</Num> deaths. The Turkish state gives about <Num s="S110">300,000</Num>, blames war, disease and revenge attacks, and rejects the word genocide. <Num s="S114,S115">Hundreds of thousands</Num> of Assyrians and Ottoman Greeks also died.

Japan's ultimatum of 15 August 1914 told Germany to hand over its leased territory on Jiaozhou Bay. China had declared itself neutral, but Japanese troops landed at Longkou, north of Tsingtao, on 2 September and moved inland along the German-built railway to Jinan; China declared the area a war zone to avoid responsibility. The Japanese force is given as about <Num s="S82">20,000</Num> men or, counting all ground troops, nearly <Num s="S81">50,000</Num>, against a German garrison of about <Num s="S82">4,000</Num> to <Num s="S81">5,000</Num>. The British lost <Num s="S82">12</Num> killed. The Germans surrendered on 7 November, and more than <Num s="S83">4,600</Num> German and Austro-Hungarian prisoners spent the rest of the war in camps in Japan.

On 18 January 1915 the Japanese minister in Beijing, Hioki Eki, handed President Yuan Shikai the Twenty-One Demands in <Num s="S85">five</Num> groups: Germany's rights in Shandong; South Manchuria and eastern Inner Mongolia; the Hanyeping iron and coal company; no coast to be leased to other powers; and Japanese advisers in China's government and police. After an ultimatum on 7 May, Yuan accepted all but the fifth group, and treaties were signed on 25 May. The Shandong question returned at the peace conference in 1919.

British and French warships failed to force the Dardanelles in March 1915, so the Entente landed an army: British, French and Indian troops at Cape Helles, the Australian and New Zealand Army Corps (Anzac) at what became Anzac Cove. The Ottoman Fifth Army held the heights. After eight months the Entente evacuated Anzac and Suvla on 19–20 December and Helles on 9 January 1916.

<More title={{ en: "The Armenian genocide: the record, two positions and the word", zh: "亚美尼亚种族灭绝：史实、两种立场与这个词" }}>

On 24 April 1915 the Ottoman police arrested Armenian writers, doctors, lawyers, journalists and political leaders in Constantinople: <Num s="S105">250</Num> that night by one count, about <Num s="S106">800</Num> in the following days by another; most were later killed. A deportation law followed on 27 May. The killing was organised by the Special Organization under leaders of the ruling Committee of Union and Progress, with the interior minister, Mehmed Talat, among the chief instigators. Deportees who reached Deir ez-Zor in the Syrian desert were massacred in the second half of 1916: about <Num s="S107">192,750</Num> people, by the authorities' own count. In 1919 an Ottoman court martial sentenced Talat, Enver, Cemal and Dr Nâzım to death in their absence.

Most historians, the International Association of Genocide Scholars and the governments or parliaments of <Num s="S112">33</Num> states, among them the United States (2021) and Germany (2016), call these events a genocide. Raphael Lemkin, who coined the word in <Num s="S113">1942</Num>, said the fate of the Armenians shaped his work; the United Nations adopted the Genocide Convention on 9 December 1948.

The Turkish state says Armenians near the war zone were relocated to the south, that deaths came from revenge attacks, banditry, famine and epidemics in a collapsing state, that there was no plan to destroy them and that more Muslims died in the same years. It puts Armenian losses at about <Num s="S110">300,000</Num>, calls the word genocide unfounded and has proposed a joint commission of historians. This topic uses the word genocide, following the large majority of scholarship, and sets out both positions.

The Assyrians and Syriac Christians remember 1914–18 as Sayfo, "the sword": contemporary estimates of their dead range from <Num s="S114">180,000</Num> to <Num s="S114">275,000</Num>. A Greek estimate puts Ottoman Greek deaths in labour battalions by 1918 at <Num s="S115">250,000</Num>. Armenians remember their dead each 24 April at the Tsitsernakaberd memorial in Yerevan, opened in <Num s="S116">1967</Num>.

The 5th Light Infantry, whose soldiers were Indian Muslims, was due to sail for Hong Kong on 16 February 1915. Rumours spread that it was really bound for the war against the Ottoman Empire, whose sultan had called Muslims to holy war; a local coffee-shop owner, Kassim Mansoor, and German prisoners interned nearby were also accused of turning the men against Britain. On 15 February about half the regiment mutinied, killed some of their officers and seized ammunition. They killed <Num s="S93">44</Num> British officers, soldiers and civilians and <Num s="S93">5</Num> Chinese and Malay civilians. Police, the Singapore Volunteer Corps, men sent by the Sultan of Johor and sailors from British, Japanese, French and Russian ships put the mutiny down; <Num s="S93">56</Num> mutineers were killed.

Courts martial convicted <Num s="S94">202</Num> men. Between <Num s="S94">41</Num> and <Num s="S93">47</Num> were shot, most of them in public outside Outram Prison: on 25 March a firing squad of <Num s="S95">110</Num> volunteers shot <Num s="S95">22</Num>, and one execution drew a crowd of <Num s="S93">15,000</Num>. Kassim Mansoor was hanged on 31 May. In all about <Num s="S94">124</Num> people died.

The war had already come close. The Emden, which <Num s="S89">captured or sank 25</Num> merchant ships, set fire to the oil tanks at Madras and sank the destroyer Mousquet off Penang, where over half its crew died, before HMAS Sydney caught her at the Cocos Islands on 9 November 1914: about <Num s="S90">130</Num> of Emden's crew and <Num s="S90">4</Num> Australians were killed.
