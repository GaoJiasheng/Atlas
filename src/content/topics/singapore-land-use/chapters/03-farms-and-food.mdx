<Lang en>

In 1959 Singapore had about <Num s="S102">20,000</Num> farms. Families raised animals, grew vegetables and tended fishponds and fruit trees. As housing and industry expanded, farmland became smaller. CLC reports that about <Num s="S101">8,000</Num> farms closed between 1975 and 1984. Losing a farm meant more than losing a patch of soil: it could end a family business and change ties between neighbours.

Water also shaped farming policy. Waste from pig farms could pollute water flowing towards reservoirs. In March 1984 the government announced that pig farming would be phased out. It cited treatment costs, land, water and nearby homes. Farmers who had invested in new facilities faced another change of direction. CLC dates the last phase-out to 1989.

From 1986 land was set aside for <Term id="agrotechnology-park">agrotechnology parks</Term>. Farms used machinery and controlled growing conditions to produce more from limited space. In <Term id="hydroponics">hydroponics</Term>, roots receive nutrients in water instead of soil. Indoor farming also uses lamps and stacked growing trays. Technology changes where and how crops can grow, though equipment and electricity still cost money.

In 2024 Singapore had <Num s="S202">153</Num> land-based and <Num s="S202">72</Num> sea-based food farms. It still imports over <Num s="S104">90%</Num> of its food. Local production is one part of food resilience, alongside reliable imports. The farm-park shapes show today’s agricultural land, approximately, rather than the boundaries of farms that disappeared.

Singapore Food Story 2, announced in November 2025, instead aims by 2035 [S220] for capacity supplying <Num s="S201,S220">20%</Num> of selected fibre foods and <Num s="S201,S220">30%</Num> of selected protein foods.
