<Lang en>

Singapore can change the way it uses land, and it can also add land. <Term id="land-reclamation">Land reclamation</Term> turns shallow sea into land by enclosing an area and filling it. East Coast reclamation began in 1966. Other projects extended Changi, Marina Bay, Jurong and Tuas. The dashed coast helps show where the shoreline has moved.

Jurong Island officially opened on 14 October 2000. Reclamation joined <Num s="S208">seven</Num> islands for oil and chemical plants. The opening did not mean that every reclamation phase had finished. Different sources give later completion dates for works with different scopes. The satellite snapshots show land on particular days, rather than the exact day each project became usable.

Space can also be found below and above the surface. Jurong Rock Caverns opened on 2 September 2014 to store liquid hydrocarbons underground. Deep tunnels carry used water towards treatment plants. Taller housing puts more homes on a building site: Pinnacle@Duxton, completed in 2009, has <Num s="S51">50</Num>-storey blocks. Building higher still requires lifts, services and shared outdoor space.

Official land-area figures rose from <Num s="S200">581.5</Num> square kilometres at the end of 1960 to <Num s="S200">735.7</Num> at the end of 2024. The calculation method changed in 2002, so the figures are not perfectly comparable. More land also involves choices: filling the sea alters coastal habitats, and sand must come from somewhere.

Dykes contain the fill. Sand and other material are placed inside, and the ground must settle and be improved before many buildings can be constructed [S207]. A new-looking shore in a satellite image is therefore not proof that the site was ready for occupation. Fill can come from local excavation or imports. NLB notes limits including imported-sand costs and damage to ecosystems [S207]. Singapore’s foreign ministry distinguishes Indonesia’s sea-sand ban in 2003 from its land-sand ban in 2007, which affected construction [S209]. Reclamation adds useful space while changing the coast and requiring material from elsewhere.

</More>

<More title={{"en": "Reading the coast carefully", "zh": "【待译】Reading the coast carefully"}}>

Water reflects green and infrared light differently from land. Comparing those reflections helps distinguish land from water. Cloud, tide and mixed coastal pixels limit the result. Early MSS images have coarser pixels than later Landsat images. Each shore is an approximate snapshot of a particular day. The older shores copied from historical maps are estimates too; neither should be read as an exact survey.

</More>
