<Lang en>

Look at the river mouth on the southern coast. The City of Singapore spreads around it, with shops, homes and the port close together. Away from the city, much of the island is countryside. <Term id="kampong">Kampong</Term> is a Malay word for village. Villages stand among vegetable farms, fishponds and rubber and coconut estates. According to the Singapore Food Agency, Singapore had about <Num s="S102">20,000</Num> farms in 1959.

The island also has places with very different purposes. In the north lies the British naval base. Airfields occupy land at Seletar, Tengah, Sembawang, Changi and Paya Lebar. Near the middle, forest surrounds reservoirs that store water for the town. The same island must provide homes, food, water, work and places to travel through.

This is a map of <Term id="land-use">land use</Term>: what land is used for. Its broad shapes come from a British government map published in April 1959. The countryside colour groups villages, farms and plantations together. It does not mean that every patch of that colour was a farm, or that every village had the same living conditions.

The dashed line marks today's coast. Where it lies outside the old shore, land was later added by filling the sea. Some village houses had piped water and electricity; others relied on shared supplies. NLB describes services reaching Kampong Wak Sumang during the 1960s [S64]. An <Term id="attap-house">attap house</Term> has a palm-leaf roof. “Kampong” names a village, rather than a single standard of housing.
