<Lang en>

Long before Zheng He sailed in 1405, ships carried goods across Asian seas. The Tang shipwreck displayed at Singapore's Asian Civilisations Museum dates to the ninth century. Its cargo makes distant exchange visible through objects that survived underwater. The ship was probably Arab-built; its final departure port in China remains uncertain. It cannot supply a complete sailing track. [S8, S9]

In the Singapore region, ceramics, coins and other finds at Fort Canning point to an early trading settlement. The name <Term id="term-temasek">Temasek</Term> also appears in a later chart tradition preserving earlier sailing knowledge. These are different kinds of <Term id="term-evidence">historical evidence</Term>: objects show exchange, while a place-name shows what a chart recorded. Neither proves that Zheng He personally landed here. <TopicLink slug="singapore-1819-1965" ch="background">Early Singapore</TopicLink> follows the settlement evidence further. [S3]

<FlyTo preset="view-straits"><Term id="term-melaka">Melaka</Term> and the straits</FlyTo> belonged to a Malay trading world with local rulers and merchants. Farther west, India's southwest-coast ports joined wider exchanges. Across the ocean, Kilwa's merchants traded goods including Chinese porcelain. These places had their own histories and interests before a Ming fleet arrived. <TopicLink slug="ancient-civilisations" ch="southeast-asia-seas">Southeast Asia's connected seas</TopicLink> gives a longer view. [S1, S6, S7, S26]

This map uses modern coastlines and sourced city or regional points to help locate places. A point can mark a trading context, rather than a confirmed fleet visit. Moving the camera changes the view; it does not recreate a ship's movement. No measured tracks or reconstructed political borders are shown. [S21, S22, S23]

<More title={{ en: "What an object can establish", zh: "器物能说明什么" }}>

A Chinese ceramic found in Singapore or Kilwa supports a history of exchange. By itself, it does not identify the ship, merchant or admiral that carried it. Archaeology and written accounts answer overlapping questions, but they cannot simply replace each other. [S3, S6, S17]

</More>

<More title={{ en: "Ports had their own purposes", zh: "港口也有自己的打算" }}>

Melaka's rulers and merchants belonged to a Malay port society. Kilwa was part of Swahili coastal culture, with connections to Arabia, India and China. Their histories include decisions made locally as well as encounters with visitors. [S7, S26]

</More>

<More title={{ en: "Many networks across the sea", zh: "海上不只有一条路" }}>

“Maritime Silk Routes” is used here as a broad name for networks of exchange. The Tang wreck and later port evidence belong to different periods. Joining their locations with a line would not recover either voyage. The route-map file sought during preparation could not be downloaded, so this topic supplies no route geometry. [S8, S9, S21]

</More>

<Lang zh>

郑和出海以前，亚洲海上已有往来的船只。新加坡亚洲文明博物馆展出的唐代沉船，年代早至九世纪。船上的货物让人看到远距离交换留下的实物。这艘船很可能由阿拉伯工匠建造，但最后从中国哪个港口出发，仍不能确定；不能据此画出完整航迹。[S8, S9]

福康宁出土的陶瓷、钱币等，说明新加坡区域早有贸易聚落。<Term id="term-temasek">淡马锡</Term>的名字又见于保存较早航海知识的后出地图。器物与地名都是<Term id="term-evidence">历史证据</Term>，却回答不同问题：前者说明物品流通，后者说明地图记了什么，都不能单独证明郑和亲自登陆。<TopicLink slug="singapore-1819-1965" ch="background">早期新加坡</TopicLink>继续介绍这些发现。[S3]

把镜头移向<FlyTo preset="view-straits"><Term id="term-melaka">马六甲</Term>与海峡</FlyTo>，当地统治者和商人有自己的利益。印度西南岸港口也参与交换；海的另一边，基尔瓦商人交易的商品包括中国瓷器。海洋网络并不从明朝船队开始。<TopicLink slug="ancient-civilisations" ch="southeast-asia-seas">东南亚的海上交流</TopicLink>把时间拉得更长。[S1, S6, S7, S26]

本图用现代海岸与有来源的城市、区域点帮助定位。地点可能只是贸易背景，未必是确认停靠处。镜头移动只改变观看位置，不代表船只走过；图中没有测得的航迹，也没有推测的历史国界。[S21, S22, S23]

<More title={{ en: "What an object can establish", zh: "器物能说明什么" }}>

在新加坡或基尔瓦发现中国陶瓷，可以支持当地存在交换活动的判断。但仅凭陶瓷，无法知道是哪艘船、哪位商人或统帅把它带来。出土物与文献需要相互比较，不能随意替代。[S3, S6, S17]

</More>

<More title={{ en: "Ports had their own purposes", zh: "港口也有自己的打算" }}>

马六甲是马来港口社会，基尔瓦属于斯瓦希里沿海文化，与阿拉伯、印度和中国都有贸易联系。当地历史也由当地人的选择构成，不能只围着来访船队讲述。[S7, S26]

</More>
