<Lang en>

You have changed between two units and one smaller unit. This step goes further: if you know tenths and hundredths, you can read scales that are marked in decimals.

Look at <Beat n={1}>the ten parts between the numbered marks</Beat>. On this <Term id="scale">scale</Term>, from <Qty u="1 kg" /> to <Qty u="2 kg" />, ten equal parts share one <Term id="kilogram">kilogram</Term>. Each <Term id="small-part">small part</Term> stands for <Qty u="0.1 kg" />, one tenth of a kilogram. It is the same method as before: the difference between two numbered marks, divided by the number of parts.

Now <Beat n={2}>count from 1 to the flour's needle</Beat>. It is four parts past <Qty u="1 kg" />, so read <Qty u="1.4 kg" />. One kilogram is <Qty u="1000 g" />, so each tenth is <Qty u="100 g" />. Four tenths are <Qty u="400 g" />, which makes <Qty u="1 kg 400 g" />, or <Qty u="1400 g" />. Reading the 4 after the point as <Qty u="4 g" /> is a mistake: it counts tenths of a kilogram.

Next, <Beat n={3}>read the jug's numbered marks</Beat>. They go up in half litres: <Qty u="0.5 l" />, <Qty u="1 l" />, <Qty u="1.5 l" />. Five equal parts share half a <Term id="litre">litre</Term>, so each part stands for <Qty u="0.1 l" />. Look from the side, with your eyes level with the water. It is three parts above one litre: <Qty u="1.3 l" />, which is <Qty u="1300 ml" />.

Finally, <Beat n={4}>look closely at Mei Ling's height chart</Beat>. Between <Qty u="1.2 m" /> and <Qty u="1.3 m" />, ten parts share one tenth of a <Term id="metre">metre</Term>, so each part stands for <Qty u="0.01 m" />, which is <Qty u="1 cm" />. Her head board is eight parts above <Qty u="1.2 m" />: <Qty u="1.28 m" />, the same as the <Qty u="128 cm" /> you read in step 03.
