<More title={{"en": "Air resistance", "zh": "空气阻力"}}>

The push of the air on something moving through it is called air resistance. It grows as the object moves faster, so a flat sheet soon stops speeding up: in this model it falls at a steady speed of about <Num s="S1">1.1 m/s</Num>. For the steel balls the air's push is tiny compared with their weight. Real paper tips, flutters and slides sideways, so its real fall is less regular than the model's.

<More title={{"en": "The hammer and the feather", "zh": "锤子和羽毛"}}>

On the Moon there is almost no air. In 1971 the Apollo 15 astronaut David Scott let go of a <Num s="S11">1.32 kg</Num> aluminium hammer and a falcon feather at the same moment. With no air to push back on the feather, they reached the ground together.

</More>

<More title={{"en": "Mass and weight", "zh": "质量和重量"}}>

Mass is measured in grams or kilograms, and it stays the same wherever the object is. Weight is a force, measured in newtons. On the Moon gravity pulls about <Num s="S10">0.165</Num> times as hard as on the Earth, so the big ball would still have a mass of <Num s="S1">224 g</Num> but would weigh only about <Num s="S1">0.4 N</Num>. On the Earth each kilogram weighs about <Num s="S8">9.8 N</Num>. Measuring forces in newtons and comparing mass with weight come again in lower secondary science.

</More>

<More title={{"en": "Forces in a lift", "zh": "电梯里的力"}}>

In a lift, steel ropes run over a grooved wheel at the top of the shaft. One end holds the lift car and the other a heavy counterweight that slides up and down its own rails. The ropes pull the car up while gravity pulls it down. Even when the car stands still at a floor, both forces act on it.
