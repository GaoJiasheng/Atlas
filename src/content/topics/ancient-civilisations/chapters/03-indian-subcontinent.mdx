<Lang en>

The Indus and several smaller rivers fed by the <Term id="monsoon">monsoon</Term> flowed across a wide, flat plain, and their yearly floods watered the fields. By about 2600 BCE planned brick cities stood there. By 2008, <Num s="S201">1,022</Num> sites of this civilisation were known, in today's Pakistan and north-west India. Mohenjo-daro and Harappa were the largest, each home to <Num s="S200,S201">tens of thousands</Num> of people by modern estimates. At Mohenjo-daro the lower town followed strict rules, and houses and streets had drains. Farmers grew wheat and barley, some of the earliest known traces of cotton come from these cities, and goods travelled as far as Mesopotamia.

People here carved short rows of signs on seals and pots. This <Term id="indus-script">Indus script</Term> has never been read. From about 1900 BCE the great cities declined, and by about 1700 BCE they had come to an end. Why is still debated.

Later, kingdoms grew up on the Ganges plain. About 321 BCE the Maurya dynasty came to power at Pataliputra, and it became the first empire to cover most of the subcontinent. King Ashoka conquered Kalinga, on the east coast. In his own inscriptions he counted the people killed and carried away, and wrote that he regretted the war.

From the early 4th century CE the Gupta kings ruled much of northern India, and about 499 the mathematician Aryabhata finished his book near their capital. Taxila, in the north-west, had been a centre of Buddhist learning since about the 5th century BCE; at Nalanda, on the Ganges plain, monks studied until the 13th century CE. How the numbers written today got their zero is told in chapter 08.

<More title={{"en": "Rivers, points and a disputed name", "zh": "河流、地点和一个有争议的名字"}}>

The cities lived on the Indus and on rivers fed by monsoon rain. A study of old river beds published in 2012 found that in the Harappan heartland only monsoon-fed rivers flowed after the last ice age; as the monsoon weakened, they dried up or ran only in some seasons, and places along them became harder to live in.

The civilisation is usually called the Indus or Harappan civilisation, after the site where it was first identified. Some authors add "Sarasvati", the name of a river in the Rigveda; which river that was is disputed.

The map shows the cities as points. The Cliopatria dataset does draw an outline for this civilisation, but for 2600 BCE it covers only about <Num s="S239">15,000</Num> square kilometres around a few cities, far less than the area where the known sites lie.

<More title={{"en": "A script no one can read yet", "zh": "一种还读不懂的文字"}}>

About <Num s="S206">4,000</Num> inscriptions are known. They are short: on average <Num s="S206">5</Num> signs, the longest <Num s="S206">17</Num>, drawn from about <Num s="S206">400</Num> different signs. No inscription in this script and a known language side by side has been found.

Scholars disagree about what the signs are. Rajesh Rao and his colleagues argue that their patterns look like the writing of a language. Steve Farmer, Richard Sproat and Michael Witzel argue that they are symbols not tied to any language. Until longer texts or a bilingual inscription turn up, no proposed reading can be checked.

</More>

<More title={{"en": "Kalinga: the king's own numbers", "zh": "羯陵伽：国王自己写下的数字"}}>

In his Thirteenth Rock Edict, Ashoka says that when he conquered Kalinga, eight years after he was anointed king, <Num s="S210,S211">150,000</Num> people were carried away and <Num s="S210,S211">100,000</Num> were killed, "and many times as many" died. He writes that Brahmans, ascetics and householders suffered too, and that he now feels deep remorse.

These are the victor's words. No record from the people of Kalinga is known. The year is given only as "eight years" into his reign; about 260 BCE is a modern reckoning.

At Dhauli and Jaugada, in Kalinga itself, the series of edicts leaves out the Thirteenth and two others. In their place are two special edicts addressed to the officials who governed the conquered land.

</More>

<Lang zh>

印度河和几条靠<Term id="monsoon">季风</Term>雨水补给的河流，流过一片宽广平坦的平原，每年的泛滥滋润着田地。约公元前 2600 年，这里已经有规划整齐的砖城。到 2008 年，已知这一文明的遗址有 <Num s="S201">1,022</Num> 处，分布在今天的巴基斯坦和印度西北部。最大的两座城是摩亨佐-达罗和哈拉帕，按现代学者的估计，各住着<Num s="S200,S201">几万</Num>人。摩亨佐-达罗的下城按严格的规则布局，房屋和街道都有排水沟。农民种小麦和大麦，已知最早的棉的痕迹之一就出自这些城市，货物一直运到美索不达米亚。

人们在印章和陶器上刻下一行行短短的符号。这种<Term id="indus-script">印度河文字</Term>至今没有人读懂。约公元前 1900 年起，大城市逐渐衰落，到约公元前 1700 年已告终，原因至今仍有争论。

后来，恒河平原上兴起了新的王国。约公元前 321 年，孔雀王朝在华氏城建立，成为第一个统治次大陆大部分地方的帝国。阿育王征服了东海岸的羯陵伽，他在自己的法敕里写下被杀、被掳的人数，并说自己后悔这场战争。

4 世纪初起，笈多王朝统治印度北部的大片地方；约 499 年，数学家阿耶波多在都城附近写成他的著作。西北的塔克西拉，早在公元前 5 世纪前后就是佛教的学术中心；恒河平原上的那烂陀，僧人在那里研习，一直延续到 13 世纪。今天写数字用的零是怎么来的，见第 08 章。

<More title={{"en": "Rivers, points and a disputed name", "zh": "河流、地点和一个有争议的名字"}}>

这些城市靠印度河和季风雨水补给的河流生存。2012 年发表的一项古河床研究发现，末次冰期结束以后，哈拉帕文明的核心地带只有靠季风补给的河流；季风减弱后，这些河逐渐干涸或变成季节河，沿河一带也越来越难住人。

这一文明通常叫“印度河文明”或“哈拉帕文明”，后者得名于最早认出这一文明的遗址。有作者再加上“萨拉斯瓦蒂”，那是《梨俱吠陀》里一条河的名字；这条河究竟是今天的哪条河，学界有争议。

地图上把城市画成点。Cliopatria 数据集确实给这一文明画了范围，但公元前 2600 年的范围只有约 <Num s="S239">1.5 万</Num>平方公里，围着几座城市，远远小于已知遗址分布的地方。

<More title={{"en": "A script no one can read yet", "zh": "一种还读不懂的文字"}}>

已知的刻文约有 <Num s="S206">4,000</Num> 件，都很短：平均 <Num s="S206">5</Num> 个符号，最长的 <Num s="S206">17</Num> 个，用到的符号约 <Num s="S206">400</Num> 种。至今没有找到用这种文字和一种已知文字对照书写的刻文。

学者对这些符号是什么意见不一。拉杰什·拉奥等人认为，符号的排列规律像是在记录一种语言；法默、斯普罗特和维策尔则认为，它们是不对应任何语言的标记。在找到更长的文本或双语刻文之前，任何一种“破译”都无法验证。

</More>

<More title={{"en": "Kalinga: the king's own numbers", "zh": "羯陵伽：国王自己写下的数字"}}>

阿育王在第十三摩崖法敕里说：他在灌顶后第八年征服羯陵伽，<Num s="S210,S211">15 万</Num>人被掳走，<Num s="S210,S211">10 万</Num>人被杀，“死去的人还多出许多倍”。他写道，婆罗门、苦行者和居家的百姓也一同受害，他如今深感悔恨。

这些是胜利者自己的话，羯陵伽人没有留下任何记录。法敕只说“灌顶后第八年”，“约公元前 260 年”是后人的推算。

在羯陵伽本地的道里和 Jaugada，刻下的法敕少了第十三章和另外两章，换成两道专门写给治理这片被征服土地的官员的敕令。

</More>
