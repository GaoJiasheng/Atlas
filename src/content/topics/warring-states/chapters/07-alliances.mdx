<Lang en>

Wei's attack on Zhao brought Qi into the struggle for the central plain. Qi defeated Wei at Guiling in 353 BCE and at Maling in 342 BCE. The Shiji connects these victories with Sun Bin, Sūn Bìn 孙膑, and Tian Ji, Tián Jì 田忌. Its account made an attack towards Wei's homeland a model for relieving pressure on an ally.

Advisers proposed <Term id="vertical-alliance">vertical alliances</Term> among states resisting Qin and <Term id="horizontal-alliance">horizontal alliances</Term> linking individual states to Qin. Neither arrangement was permanent. Zhang Yi, Zhāng Yí 张仪, used diplomacy to separate Chu from Qi. In 312 BCE, Qin defeated Chu at Danyang and again near Lantian. The Shiji's account of a promised land grant illustrates how later writers represented deception in diplomacy.

Qin conquered Ba and Shu in 316 BCE, gaining access to the Sichuan basin. Qi attacked Yan during its internal crisis in 314 BCE. The Mencius condemns the killing and taking of captives that followed: expansion by a rival of Qin also carried human costs. In Zhao, King Wuling, Zhào Wǔlíng Wáng 赵武灵王, promoted northern dress and mounted archery in 307 BCE. Zhao conquered Zhongshan in 296 BCE, ending another state's independent rule.

Qin defeated Han and Wei at Yique in 293 BCE. Yet Qi then faced a coalition led by Yan in 284 BCE, and much of its territory was occupied. Military strength had not secured it against changing partnerships. At courts including Qi's Jixia, Mencius, Mèngzǐ 孟子, and other thinkers debated rulers' responsibilities. Their proposals addressed the same competition for people and resources that repeatedly sent armies across the map.

<More title={{"en": "Su Qin, Zhang Yi and a changed chronology", "zh": "【待译】Su Qin, Zhang Yi and a changed chronology"}}>

The Shiji places Su Qin, Sū Qín 苏秦, before Zhang Yi and presents his career as a model of coalition diplomacy. This arrangement helped generations of readers contrast the advocates of vertical and horizontal alliances.

Silk manuscripts excavated at Mawangdui in 1973 CE place much of Su Qin's documented activity around 300–284 BCE, later than Zhang Yi's main career. The manuscripts change the chronology without making every transmitted speech worthless: those speeches also show how later compilers explained persuasion and political choice.

</More>

<More title={{"en": "Pang Juan in different texts", "zh": "【待译】Pang Juan in different texts"}}>

The Shiji locates Pang Juan's death in its account of Maling. A military text excavated at Yinqueshan, usually titled “Capturing Pang Juan,” instead associates his capture with the Guiling campaign. These accounts cannot simply be combined into a single securely documented biography. The <Term id="crossbow">crossbow</Term> is prominent in the Shiji's Maling narrative, but its dramatic ambush scene is a literary account, not an archaeological count of the weapons used.

</More>

<More title={{"en": "Learning and conquering on the northern frontier", "zh": "【待译】Learning and conquering on the northern frontier"}}>

Zhao's adoption of mounted archery drew on practices associated with northern neighbours. The policy involved argument within Zhao's court about dress, custom and military effectiveness. Communities called Linhu and Loufan appear mainly through the records of states expanding into their lands. Their experiences cannot be reconstructed by inventing speeches for them. Zhongshan, associated with people described as White Di, was itself an organised state with a court and a material culture.

</More>

<More title={{"en": "Walls and arguments about warfare", "zh": "【待译】Walls and arguments about warfare"}}>

Qi, Chu, Wei, Zhao, Yan and Qin built defensive walls at different times. Many faced neighbouring states rather than northern pastoral communities. Rammed-earth remains are evidence for construction and labour; a later Ming wall cannot stand in for them. No unverified ancient wall line is drawn here. At Jixia and other courts, scholars debated how rulers should govern. The Mencius' criticism of warfare challenges the assumption that the largest territorial gain necessarily produced the best government.

</More>
