<Lang en>

In 221 BCE, Ying Zheng adopted the title First Emperor. Qin replaced rival royal courts with administration through commanderies and counties. The Shiji describes an initial division into <Num s="S150">36</Num> commanderies, though their number and boundaries changed. Measures to standardise writing, weights, measures and transport helped officials communicate and collect resources across conquered territories. Implementation depended on local workers, clerks and communities.

Roads, walls, palaces and the imperial tomb required labour. Campaigns in 214 BCE took lands south of the northern frontier and in Lingnan; the Lingqu canal connected waterways used in the southern operations. These later expansions are discussed here but are not added to the map without a suitable territorial source. Its final frame remains an approximate reconstruction of the states annexed by 221 BCE.

In 213 BCE, the government ordered books destroyed, with specified exemptions. The Shiji reports the killing of more than <Num s="S150">460</Num> men described as zhusheng in 212 BCE. Whether all were Confucian scholars, and how accurately the account represents the episode, remain disputed. Describing the uncertainty does not turn a reported killing into an administrative achievement. Excavated legal documents also reveal routine demands, punishments and disputes affecting people whose names seldom appear in political histories.

The emperor died at Shaqiu in 210 BCE. Chen Sheng, Chén Shèng 陈胜, and Wu Guang, Wú Guǎng 吴广, led an uprising in 209 BCE. Ziying, Zǐyīng 子婴, surrendered in the year conventionally called 206 BCE; calendar conventions also produce 207 BCE. Han writers criticised Qin while later governments retained important institutions. The empire's short life and long administrative legacy require explanation together, without treating either as a verdict on every person who lived under it.

<More title={{"en": "Irrigation and material evidence", "zh": "【待译】Irrigation and material evidence"}}>

The Shiji credits Li Bing, Lǐ Bīng 李冰, with water works in Shu. Dujiangyan's ancient beginnings lie in the third century BCE, but the narrower construction range of 256–251 BCE remains [待核] here. Its surviving works have been altered repeatedly and should not be treated as an unchanged plan from Qin times. The Zhengguo canal's conventional beginning is 246 BCE. Irrigation increased productive capacity while requiring organisation and labour; later engineering success does not make the costs of construction disappear.

</More>

<More title={{"en": "People in Qin’s paperwork", "zh": "【待译】People in Qin’s paperwork"}}>

Bamboo slips from Shuihudi and documents from Liye show officials handling land, labour, legal liability and reporting. They offer perspectives different from a biography of an emperor. The Shiji reports more than <Num s="S150">700,000</Num> people assigned to work associated with the palace and mausoleum. The figure is a transmitted claim, not an archaeological census. Its account also describes seized weapons cast into <Num s="S150">12</Num> metal figures, another statement about imperial authority that requires attribution.

</More>

<More title={{"en": "Walls and later legend", "zh": "【待译】Walls and later legend"}}>

The stories now known as Meng Jiangnü developed over a long period; the early account of a woman mourning her husband does not contain the complete later story of the Qin wall collapsing. Walls had been built by several rival states before Qin, and later dynasties rebuilt them. Neither the familiar legend nor a surviving Ming structure can serve as direct geographical evidence for a Qin wall line.

</More>

<More title={{"en": "Criticism, inheritance and remembrance", "zh": "【待译】Criticism, inheritance and remembrance"}}>

Jia Yi, Jiǎ Yì 贾谊, in his essay on Qin argued that conquering and governing required different policies. That is a Han-era explanation shaped by its own political concerns. Institutional histories emphasise the continuity of appointed administration, while archaeological documents reveal how policies worked in practice. The terracotta army was discovered in 1974 CE. UNESCO inscribed the First Emperor's mausoleum in 1987 CE and Qingcheng Mountain with Dujiangyan in 2000 CE. Their modern recognition preserves material evidence; it does not settle every judgment about the governments that created them.

</More>
