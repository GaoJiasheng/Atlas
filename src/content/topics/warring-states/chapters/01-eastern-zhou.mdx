<Lang en>

King Ping of Zhou, Zhōu Píng Wáng 周平王, established his court at Luoyi, near present-day Luoyang. The move is traditionally dated to 770 BCE. The king retained ritual authority, but directly controlled less land and depended on neighbouring lords for protection and supplies. Recent readings of excavated texts suggest a more prolonged struggle over the succession than the familiar account of an orderly move implies.

Qin and Zheng benefited in different ways. According to the Shiji, Duke Xiang of Qin, Qín Xiāng Gōng 秦襄公, helped escort the king east and received recognition as a regional lord. Qin then extended its authority into former royal lands in the west. Zheng, closer to the new royal centre, became an important military partner and a competitor for land and influence.

Under Duke Zhuang of Zheng, Zhèng Zhuāng Gōng 郑庄公, cooperation with the king gave way to open conflict. The Zuo Tradition describes royal and Zheng princes being exchanged as hostages. The exchange reveals how little shared ancestry and ceremonial rank could guarantee trust when rulers depended on each other's cooperation.

In 707 BCE, King Huan of Zhou, Zhōu Huán Wáng 周桓王, led a coalition against Zheng at Xuge. Zheng's forces defeated it, and the text says an arrow struck the king's shoulder. The royal title survived the defeat, but could no longer ensure obedience. Meanwhile, records kept in Lu begin the surviving Spring and Autumn Annals in 722 BCE. Their attention to local successions, meetings and warfare provides a different view of the same political world, seen from a smaller court.

<More title={{"en": "A disputed royal succession", "zh": "【待译】A disputed royal succession"}}>

The excavated Xinian describes rival kings and a period without a generally accepted Zhou ruler. It complicates the Shiji's sequence and the conventional date of the move. The topic keeps 770 BCE as the traditional reference point while distinguishing it from a date established beyond dispute. Li Feng and Yuri Pines discuss how transmitted and excavated accounts can be reconciled; they do not make every detail certain.

</More>

<More title={{"en": "Why “Spring and Autumn”?", "zh": "【待译】Why “Spring and Autumn”?"}}>

The name comes from Lu's annals, which arrange entries by year and season. The surviving text runs from 722 BCE to 481 BCE. Modern period divisions often use 770–476 BCE, but the title of a book and the boundaries of a historical period answer different questions. A period label should not suggest that people abruptly changed how they lived at its endpoint.

</More>

<More title={{"en": "Hostages and royal authority", "zh": "【待译】Hostages and royal authority"}}>

The Zuo Tradition's account of the hostage exchange belongs to its discussion of the breakdown of trust between Zhou and Zheng. A royal son and a lord's son could become guarantees for an agreement. This was a relationship between courts with unequal ceremonial standing but military power that the king could not simply command. The people supporting their armies are less visible in the narrative.

</More>
