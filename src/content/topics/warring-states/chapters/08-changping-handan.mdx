<Lang en>

Qin expanded against Chu before turning towards Zhao. Bai Qi, Bái Qǐ 白起, captured Yan in 279 BCE and Ying in 278 BCE, forcing Chu's court east to Chen. A much later account describes flooding at Yan with mass deaths. Meanwhile, Tian Dan, Tián Dān 田单, restored Qi's position against Yan. Qin won at Huayang in 273 BCE but lost to Zhao at Yuyu in 269 BCE. Its expansion remained contested.

In 262 BCE, Qin cut the connection between Han's Shangdang territory and its capital. The governor Feng Ting, Féng Tíng 冯亭, offered Shangdang to Zhao. Qin and Zhao then fought around Changping. Zhao replaced Lian Po, Lián Pō 廉颇, with Zhao Kuo, Zhào Kuò 赵括; Qin secretly appointed Bai Qi. The Shiji describes Zhao's army being surrounded and deprived of food for <Num s="S152">46</Num> days.

Zhao Kuo died attempting a breakout. The survivors surrendered, and Qin killed the prisoners, releasing only <Num s="S152">240</Num> younger captives according to the biography. The Shiji gives more than <Num s="S151">400,000</Num> in its Qin annals and <Num s="S152">450,000</Num> beheaded or captured across the campaign in Bai Qi's biography. These are disputed transmitted records, not a modern count of bodies. Most victims left no surviving names; remembering them keeps the consequences of state expansion visible.

Qin besieged Handan from 259 BCE to 257 BCE. Relief forces from Wei and Chu helped Zhao resist, and Qin withdrew. Bai Qi was ordered to die in 257 BCE. Qin conquered the western Zhou royal domain in 256 BCE, when King Nan of Zhou, Zhōu Nǎn Wáng 周赧王, died. The end of the royal house did not yet mean the end of independent states.

<More title={{"en": "Changping: text, remains and uncertainty", "zh": "【待译】Changping: text, remains and uncertainty"}}>

The Shiji's large totals may combine phases of the campaign. Questions about population, recruitment and food supply also make their scale disputed. Specific modern scholars' demographic calculations and page references remain [待核]; no smaller total is substituted here. The text attributes to Bai Qi the statement that more than <Num s="S152">half</Num> of Qin's soldiers had died, but supplies no secure Qin total.

The local heritage protection plan reports approximately <Num s="S158">130</Num> individuals in the Yonglu burial pit investigated in 1995 CE. The remains provide material evidence of mass death at the site, without establishing the total killed across the whole campaign or the precise circumstances of every death. The original excavation report's complete text and osteological conclusions remain [待核]. “Keng” is therefore not translated automatically as burying every prisoner alive.

</More>

<More title={{"en": "Water attacks and occupation", "zh": "【待译】Water attacks and occupation"}}>

The Shiji records the capture of Yan without a death total. The sixth-century Shuijing zhu describes flooding and claims <Num s="S11">hundreds of thousands</Num> of deaths: its distance from the event matters, and the number is not used as a secure count. The Shiji also recounts Qi's opponents mutilating surrendered soldiers and disturbing tombs during the occupation of Qi, within a story about Tian Dan provoking resistance. Attribution matters here just as it does for Qin's violence.

</More>

<More title={{"en": "The tally and the relief of Handan", "zh": "【待译】The tally and the relief of Handan"}}>

A <Term id="tiger-tally">tiger tally</Term> was divided so that matching parts could authorise a military order. The Shiji says Lord Xinling, Xìnlíng Jūn 信陵君, obtained Wei's tally with Ru Ji, Rú Jī 如姬, helping him, killed the commander Jin Bi, Jìn Bǐ 晋鄙, and took his army to relieve Zhao. Lord Chunshen, Chūnshēn Jūn 春申君, led Chu's assistance. This account of rescue also includes coercion and a killing within the rescuing force; they should remain visible alongside the relief of Handan.

</More>
