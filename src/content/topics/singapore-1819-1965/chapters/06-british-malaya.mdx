<Lang en>

“British Malaya” described a group of territories, not a single country with one government. Singapore, Penang and Malacca belonged to the Straits Settlements. In Perak, the Pangkor agreement of 1874 placed a British <Term id="resident">Resident</Term> beside the Sultan. The arrangement extended British influence through advice that the ruler was expected to follow, with exceptions for Malay religion and custom. Malay rulers retained titles and institutions while losing much practical control over administration.

Perak, Selangor, Negeri Sembilan and Pahang formed the Federated Malay States in 1896. Other states remained outside that federation. The Anglo-Siamese agreement of 1909 transferred claims over Kedah, Perlis, Kelantan and Terengganu from Siam to British protection. Johor followed a different path through treaties and advisers. Calling all these territories colonies would erase distinctions that mattered to their rulers and residents.

Tin and rubber connected the peninsula's mines and plantations with Singapore's merchants, processing businesses and port. Tamil plantation workers and Chinese mining workers helped produce these exports under unequal recruitment and labour conditions. Transport links grew around this economy. The Causeway officially opened in 1924, carrying road and railway traffic across the Johor Strait. Water supply also connected island and peninsula.

In 1915 part of an Indian regiment mutinied in Singapore. Soldiers and civilians were killed; British and supporting forces suppressed the rising, and condemned mutineers were executed publicly. The interwar economy later faced falling demand and unemployment. The naval base opened in 1938, but a large military installation did not remove the island's dependence on regional trade, workers and water.
