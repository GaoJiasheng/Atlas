<Lang en>

In 1826 Penang, Malacca and Singapore were grouped as the <Term id="straits-settlements-colony">Straits Settlements</Term>. The East India Company administered them through British India. Singapore's position linked the Straits of Malacca to routes across the South China Sea and the Indonesian archipelago. Cargo might be unloaded, stored and resold here without being produced on the island. This exchange between other ports was called entrepôt trade.

Singapore operated as a <Term id="free-trade-port">free port</Term>, without ordinary import and export duties. Chinese junks brought goods from southern China; Bugis traders linked the port with Sulawesi and other islands. Indian and European merchants carried textiles, opium and manufactured goods, taking away tin, pepper and other regional products. Sailing schedules depended on seasonal winds. The port's business belonged to a wider network rather than to a single community.

Free trade did not mean that government cost nothing. The authorities raised money through <Term id="revenue-farm">revenue farms</Term>, selling merchants exclusive rights to collect income from businesses such as opium, alcohol and gambling. These arrangements enriched some merchants and sustained public administration. They also tied the government's income to activities that caused debt and illness among workers and households.

New docks and steam shipping changed the waterfront. The Tanjong Pagar Dock Company developed deep-water facilities west of the town. In 1867 the Straits Settlements became a <Term id="crown-colony">Crown Colony</Term>, administered directly by Britain. The Suez Canal opened in 1869 and changed routes between Europe and Asia. The port expanded through commerce, but its benefits and burdens were distributed unevenly.

An opium revenue farm was a government concession, not a farm growing plants in Singapore. A merchant paid for exclusive rights and recovered that payment through sales. The share of colonial income from opium varied by year. Diana Kim’s study reports that at its nineteenth-century peak it supplied <Num s="S208">over half</Num> of Straits Settlements revenue. This does not establish the same share in every year, or measure the costs borne by individual households.
