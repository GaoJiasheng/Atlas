<Lang en>

Singapore's participation in Malaysia brought disputes over political competition, economic policy and the meaning of citizenship. PAP leaders argued for a “Malaysian Malaysia”. Alliance and UMNO leaders challenged PAP's activities on the peninsula and its claims about equality. Party speeches and newspapers sharpened communal fears. Those arguments should be attributed to their speakers rather than presented as the views of every Malay or Chinese resident.

On 21 July 1964 clashes broke out during a procession marking the Prophet Muhammad's birthday. <Num s="S156">23</Num> people died and <Num s="S156">454</Num> were injured. Further riots began in September, with <Num s="S156">13</Num> killed and <Num s="S156">106</Num> injured in the cited account. Curfews interrupted work and daily travel. Interracial goodwill committees tried to restore contact. Government and party accounts disagreed over provocation and responsibility; families experienced deaths and danger regardless of those explanations.

Indonesia's Confrontation also reached civilians. On 10 March 1965 a bomb at MacDonald House killed <Num s="S157">three</Num> people and injured <Num s="S157">33</Num>. Indonesian operatives carried out the attack. Its victims were people working in the building, not a military formation. Remembering their names keeps the event from becoming only a diplomatic dispute.

An independence agreement was signed on 7 August. On 9 August Malaysia's Parliament approved separation, and Singapore became independent. Tunku described separation as necessary to avoid further conflict; Lee publicly described a painful break with the merger he had supported. Their accounts explain their own decisions, not every resident's response. On 22 December Parliament changed the head of state’s title to President, recognising Yusof Ishak retrospectively from independence. Later remembrance includes Racial Harmony Day, while Malaysia and Singapore tell the federation's ending from different national perspectives.
