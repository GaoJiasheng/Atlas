<Lang en>

On 27 May 1961 Tunku Abdul Rahman proposed a closer association of Malaya, Singapore and the British territories in northern Borneo. Singapore's PAP government argued that <Term id="merger-1963">merger</Term> would provide an economic hinterland and complete the move from colonial rule. The federation's leaders also considered security and the population balance. Britain sought an arrangement compatible with withdrawal from colonial government and continued strategic interests.

The proposal divided Singapore's governing party. Barisan Sosialis opposed the proposed terms, arguing for a different path to union. On 1 September 1962 a referendum asked voters to choose between forms of merger. About <Num s="S193">71</Num> percent selected the government's option, while roughly <Num s="S193">a quarter</Num> cast blank votes. There was no separate option rejecting merger outright. Supporters pointed to the winning vote; opponents challenged the design of the choice.

On 2 February 1963 Operation Coldstore detained opposition politicians, unionists and students without trial. The authorities alleged a communist security threat. Detainees and some historians argue that anti-colonial opposition was being suppressed and that the operation helped remove political rivals. These accounts disagree about the evidence and purpose of the detentions. The fact of detention should be distinguished from proving the allegations against each person.

Sarawak and North Borneo had their own debates and conditions; they were not simply additions to a Singapore-Malaya dispute. Brunei did not join. Indonesia opposed the project through <Term id="konfrontasi">Konfrontasi</Term>, and the Philippines claimed Sabah. Following an agreement signed in London on 9 July, Malaysia was formed on 16 September 1963 by Malaya, Singapore, Sabah and Sarawak. Agreement on federation did not end disagreements about how it should work.

The government's explanation placed Coldstore within a regional communist security threat. Its critics question the evidence that the detained organisations were controlled by a communist conspiracy and emphasise anti-colonial mobilisation. Thum Ping Tjin emphasises anti-colonial opposition and political motives; the government’s later account argues that documentary evidence supports a communist security threat. A British parliamentary question described <Num s="S186">107</Num> detained on 2 February, while the minister’s answer gave <Num s="S186">110</Num> “on and since” that date. Those scopes differ. Neither total proves the allegations against each detained person.

The Cobbold Commission gathered views in North Borneo and Sarawak, and subsequent negotiations set conditions for entry. Opinions differed within each territory. Brunei's political conflict and armed revolt belonged to its own history as well as the federation proposal. A small-scale map can show membership but cannot represent every person's consent.

</More>

The referendum was about alternative terms of merger, not an uncomplicated yes-or-no question. Blank votes were a central part of the opposition campaign. Its result therefore needs both the recorded totals and an explanation of the choices that voters were offered.
