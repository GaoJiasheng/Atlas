<Lang en>

People reached Singapore through overlapping trading and family networks. Chinese migrants came from southern coastal provinces, speaking Hokkien, Teochew, Cantonese, Hakka, Hainanese and other languages. Some were merchants with capital or relatives to meet them. Others travelled through a <Term id="credit-ticket">credit-ticket system</Term>: a recruiter advanced their fare, leaving them to repay a debt through work. Recruiters and lodging-house operators could control a new arrival's access to employment.

Indian arrivals also had different circumstances. Tamil and Chulia merchants traded from the Coromandel coast; other migrants became shopkeepers, moneylenders, guards or labourers. Colonial authorities transported convicted prisoners to Singapore from 1825. They built roads and public buildings under compulsion. Plantation recruitment later included the <Term id="kangany">kangany system</Term>, in which a foreman recruited through connections in India. Convicts, indebted workers and independent merchants should not be described as choosing migration on equal terms.

Malay, Bugis, Javanese and Boyanese migrants moved through the archipelago. Work, commerce, pilgrimage and kinship could overlap. Hadhrami Arab families developed trading, property and religious networks. European, Armenian and Jewish merchants and Eurasian families also belonged to the port. <Term id="peranakan">Peranakan</Term> communities connected locally born families with the cultures and languages around them.

Migration could bring income and opportunities, but also debt, illness and separation. Women faced employment restrictions, trafficking and domestic dependency that a list of male occupations can conceal. In 1877 the Chinese Protectorate began regulating immigration and labour arrangements. The historical term <Term id="coolie">“coolie”</Term> is used here only with explanation: it reduced diverse people to a category of cheap physical labour.

A prepaid passage was not necessarily a gift. A migrant could owe recruitment, travel and lodging costs before earning wages. Fraud and confinement made the system coercive for some arrivals. Legislation and the Chinese Protectorate sought to regulate these practices, while employers continued to depend on migrant labour.

They distinguish abolition of transportation in 1867 from the ending of Singapore's convict establishment in 1873. Prisoners worked on roads, bridges and buildings; describing only the buildings would conceal the conditions under which they were made.

The January 1824 enumeration recorded <Num s="S4">10,683</Num> people. The formal census of 2 April 1871 counted <Num s="S4">97,111</Num>. Classification names changed across enumerations, and a category such as “Indians” could combine people with different languages, religions and occupations. A census can count a population without describing each person's life.
