<Lang en>

Shortly after midnight on 8 December 1941 Japanese troops began landing at Kota Bharu in north-east Malaya. Singapore was bombed that morning: <Num s="S37">61</Num> people were killed and <Num s="S37">133</Num> injured. Japanese forces advanced down the peninsula and crossed the Johor Strait on the night of 8 February. The Malay Regiment fought on the Pasir Panjang ridge and at Bukit Chandu, where Second Lieutenant Adnan Saidi was captured and killed on 14 February. Chinese volunteers also helped defend the island.

On 14 February Japanese soldiers killed <Num s="S38">about 200</Num> patients and staff at Alexandra Hospital. Lieutenant-General Arthur Percival signed the surrender at the Ford factory on 15 February. The island garrison numbered <Num s="S29">about 85,000</Num>. Singapore was renamed Syonan-to. In the Sook Ching operation, Chinese men were screened and those selected were taken away and killed. The Japanese figure at the 1947 trial was <Num s="S96">about 5,000</Num>; most estimates lie between <Num s="S95">25,000 and 50,000</Num>, and Chinese community estimates go up to <Num s="S95">100,000</Num>.

Occupation affected communities differently. Chinese residents faced targeted screening and compulsory payments. Japanese efforts to recruit Indian prisoners into the Indian National Army combined an appeal to end British rule with pressure to join. Malays also faced demands for labour and obedience. European civilians were interned; Eurasian families experienced displacement. Shortages, rationing and depreciating occupation money shaped daily life across communities.

Resistance included Force 136 and the Malayan People's Anti-Japanese Army. Allied air attacks also endangered civilians. On 12 September 1945 Mountbatten accepted the surrender of Japanese forces in Southeast Asia at the Municipal Building. The end of occupation restored British rule; it did not restore every family's health, livelihood or missing relatives.

The operation ran from 18 February to 4 March 1942 in the existing ww2 account. Hayashi locates concentrated urban screening mainly on 21–23 February. A Japanese army record lists <Num s="S97">11,110</Num> “missing as a result of bombing and the purge”; Hayashi questions that figure's credibility. It is not a complete casualty register. At Changi Beach <Num s="S98">66</Num> men were killed on 20 February; at Punggol about <Num s="S99">400</Num> were killed on 28 February. The different scopes and purposes of these records explain why they cannot simply be added together.

<More title={{"en":"Daily life and coercion","zh":"【待译】Daily life and coercion"}}>

Chinese residents faced screening, killings and compulsory payments. The occupation authorities demanded a tribute of <Num s="S95">50 million</Num> dollars from the Chinese community. Food shortages and declining currency affected families beyond those directly targeted in the screening operation.

Malay residents included fishermen and farmers whose food production helped sustain the island. NLB’s account of rationing records fishing communities at Punggol, Pasir Ris and Pasir Panjang. Community labels do not show whether a household had food, secure employment or access to medicine.

Indian residents included civilian workers and captured soldiers. The Indian National Army offered an anti-colonial appeal, while military authority and pressure shaped prisoners’ choices. Asian labourers sent to the Burma–Thailand Railway came from several communities; the railway death estimates must not be treated as a count of Indian Singaporeans alone.

Eurasian and Chinese Catholic families were moved to Bahau. NLB reports <Num s="S101">about 3,000</Num> settlers; its food-history article gives deaths between <Num s="S101">300 and 1,500</Num>, while its dedicated Bahau account gives <Num s="S177">about 500</Num> in 1944–1945. These accounts have different stated scopes and do not establish one exact toll. European civilians were interned, including at Changi. Neither Japanese recruitment appeals nor British accounts of resistance describe every resident’s experience.

</More>

<More title={{"en":"Allied air attacks","zh":"【待译】Allied air attacks"}}>

The official USAAF history describes <Num s="S120">53</Num> aircraft attacking the dockyard on 5 November 1944. It records further dock and oil-installation attacks into March 1945. Its assessment of the 24 February Empire Dock raid says <Num s="S120">39</Num> percent of warehouse area was destroyed. These are the attacker’s operational figures, not a count of civilian victims. People living or working near the targets also faced bombs and fire; a reliable civilian death total has not yet been verified. Japanese military installations and Singapore’s civilian population were distinct, even when they occupied adjoining parts of the port.

</More>
