<Lang en>

Singapore lies where the Straits of Malacca meet the South China Sea. Sailing ships timed their voyages by the monsoon winds, and ships going between India, the Malay world and China by the shortest sea route passed the island at the tip of the peninsula. People lived and traded here long before the British came.

In 1349 the Chinese traveller Wang Dayuan described a place he called Danmaxi and a nearby strait he called Longyamen, “Dragon's Teeth Gate”. A Javanese court poem of 1365 names Tumasik among the lands linked to Majapahit. Since 1984 archaeologists on Fort Canning Hill have found fourteenth-century Chinese porcelain, glass and other trade goods, and workmen there found gold ornaments by chance in 1928.

The Malay Annals, a royal chronicle, tell how Sang Nila Utama, a prince from Palembang, saw a strange beast he took for a lion and named the place Singapura, “lion city”. This is a legend: no lions lived here, and the date 1299 often given for it is not in the Annals. The Annals and the Portuguese writer Tomé Pires both say that Singapura's last ruler fled to Malacca around 1400, but they tell that story differently.

After the Portuguese took Malacca in 1511, its royal family fled to Pahang and then to Bintan; their descendants ruled the sultanate of Johor, from the Johor River and later from the Riau islands. The <Term id="orang-laut">Orang Laut</Term> patrolled the straits for them, under officials such as the <Term id="temenggong">Temenggong</Term>. When Sultan Mahmud III died at Lingga in 1811, his court split over who should succeed him. By 1818 Britain held Penang, and the Dutch had returned to Malacca.

<More title={{"en":"A lion, a legend and two endings","zh":"狮子、传说和两种结局"}}>

In the Malay Annals, Sang Nila Utama sails from Palembang, lands on an island called Temasek and sees an animal with a red body, black head and white breast; he is told it must be a lion and names the town Singapura. The Annals were written down centuries later for the Malacca court, and their purpose was to show where its kings came from. Later writers worked out the year 1299 by counting back through the reigns; the Annals give no such date.

The Annals end Singapura's story with a Majapahit attack and the flight of its last king, Iskandar Shah, to Malacca. Tomé Pires, writing in Malacca around 1512–15, says instead that Parameswara, a prince who fled Palembang, killed the ruler of Singapura and later moved on to found Malacca. Both accounts survive; neither can be checked against a fourteenth-century record.

</More>

<More title={{"en":"What lies under Fort Canning","zh":"福康宁山下有什么"}}>

The archaeologist John Miksic began digging on Fort Canning Hill in January 1984; the first ten-day dig recovered <Num s="S241">1,346</Num> pieces of pottery. These and later finds — Chinese porcelain and stoneware, glass and other goods — show a busy fourteenth-century port. In 1928 workers digging a reservoir there found <Num s="S11">ten</Num> gold ornaments in a Javanese style; only <Num s="S11">four</Num> survived the Japanese occupation.

The Singapore Stone was a large inscribed rock at the mouth of the river. In 1843 the British blew it up to widen the river entrance. A fragment is now in the National Museum; its script has never been fully read.

</More>

<More title={{"en":"A “sleepy fishing village”?","zh":"“沉睡的小渔村”？"}}>

Older books say that in 1819 Singapore was a sleepy fishing village. The numbers are guesses. A 1921 history put the population at <Num s="S4">150</Num>, about <Num s="S4">30</Num> of them Chinese, and Raffles wrote of some <Num s="S4">200</Num>. The historian C. M. Turnbull counted about <Num s="S351">1,000</Num>, most of them Orang Laut living on boats: some <Num s="S351">500</Num> Orang Kallang, <Num s="S351">200</Num> Orang Seletar and <Num s="S351">150</Num> Orang Gelam, with a few dozen Malays of the Temenggong's following and about as many Chinese. The gap between the figures is mostly the sea people, whom the smaller counts leave out. Historians today also stress that the island had been a port centuries before.

</More>

<More title={{"en":"How the maps were drawn","zh":"地图是怎么画的"}}>

The island's coast changes with the date. Each period's shore is traced from a georeferenced old map: around the town, Jackson's plan of 1822 (used up to 1822), Coleman's map of 1839 (1823–1886) and a town plan of 1893 (1887–1911); for the rest of the island, a War Office map surveyed in 1911–16 (to 1936), a map of 1941 (1937–60) and an Admiralty chart corrected to 1954, with an 1960–61 survey of the far west (from 1961). So the early town is drawn from later maps, the eastern Jurong shore of the 1960s comes from the older maps, and the line can be a few hundred metres out.

Some things are missing. Smaller islands, such as Blakang Mati (now Sentosa), Ubin and Tekong, are not drawn; rivers and reservoirs are today's; and the Malayan state borders are today's, corrected from an 1898 map, without the Pangkor islands of the Dindings. On the wide views of Malaya and the region, Singapore keeps its modern outline. Land reclaimed after 1965 never appears.

</More>

<Lang zh>

新加坡位于马六甲海峡与南中国海交汇的地方。帆船按季风出航，从印度经马来世界到中国，走最短的海路都要经过马来半岛南端的这座岛。早在英国人到来之前，就有人在这里生活和做买卖。

1349 年，中国人汪大渊在《岛夷志略》里记下一个叫“单马锡”的地方，还有附近一道叫“龙牙门”的海峡。1365 年成书的爪哇宫廷长诗《爪哇史颂》，把“淡马锡”列在与满者伯夷有关的地方之中。1984 年起，考古学家在福康宁山上挖出 14 世纪的中国瓷器、玻璃和其他货物；1928 年，工人在那里施工时还意外挖到几件金饰。

王室史书《马来纪年》讲，巨港王子山尼拉乌他玛看见一头奇怪的野兽，以为是狮子，就把这里叫作“新加坡拉”，意思是“狮子城”。这是传说：这里从来没有狮子，常说的“1299 年”也不见于《马来纪年》。《马来纪年》和葡萄牙人托梅·皮列士都说，新加坡拉的最后一位统治者在 1400 年前后逃往马六甲，但两边讲法不同。

1511 年葡萄牙人攻占马六甲以后，马六甲王室先逃到彭亨，再到民丹岛；他们的后代建立柔佛王朝，先在柔佛河，后来在廖内群岛立足。<Term id="orang-laut">海人</Term>替苏丹巡守海峡，由<Term id="temenggong">天猛公</Term>等官员管辖。1811 年苏丹马末沙三世在林加去世，朝廷为由谁继位而分裂。到 1818 年，英国人占着槟城，荷兰人也回到了马六甲。

<More title={{"en":"A lion, a legend and two endings","zh":"狮子、传说和两种结局"}}>

《马来纪年》里，山尼拉乌他玛从巨港出海，登上一座叫“淡马锡”的岛，看见一头身体赤红、头黑、胸白的野兽；随从说那一定是狮子，他便把这座城叫作“新加坡拉”。《马来纪年》是几百年后为马六甲王室写下的，目的是讲清楚国王们的来历。“1299 年”是后人按历代国王在位年数倒推出来的，书里并没有这个年份。

《马来纪年》说，新加坡拉亡于满者伯夷的进攻，末代国王伊斯干达沙逃到马六甲。约 1512 年至 1515 年在马六甲写作的托梅·皮列士却说，从巨港出逃的王子拜里米苏拉杀了新加坡拉的统治者，后来才去马六甲建国。两种说法都流传下来，都没有 14 世纪的记录可以对证。

</More>

<More title={{"en":"What lies under Fort Canning","zh":"福康宁山下有什么"}}>

考古学家米克锡克（John Miksic）1984 年 1 月开始在福康宁山发掘，第一次为期十天的发掘就出土了 <Num s="S241">1,346</Num> 片陶瓷碎片。这些以及后来出土的中国瓷器、陶器、玻璃等货物，说明 14 世纪这里是一个热闹的港口。1928 年，工人在山上挖蓄水池时，发现了 <Num s="S11">10</Num> 件爪哇风格的金饰，经过日据时期只剩下 <Num s="S11">4</Num> 件。

新加坡石原是河口一块刻着文字的大石头。1843 年，英国人为了拓宽河口，把它炸毁。一块残片今天藏在新加坡国家博物馆，上面的文字至今没有完全读通。

</More>

<More title={{"en":"A “sleepy fishing village”?","zh":"“沉睡的小渔村”？"}}>

旧书常说，1819 年的新加坡是一个“沉睡的小渔村”。当时的人数其实都是估计。1921 年出版的一部史书说约 <Num s="S4">150</Num> 人，其中华人约 <Num s="S4">30</Num> 人；莱佛士写的是约 <Num s="S4">200</Num> 人。史家滕布尔（C. M. Turnbull）算出的是约 <Num s="S351">1,000</Num> 人，大多是住在船上的海人：加冷海人约 <Num s="S351">500</Num>、实里达海人 <Num s="S351">200</Num>、格南海人 <Num s="S351">150</Num>，另有天猛公手下几十名马来人，华人也差不多这么多。几个数字差这么远，主要是因为较小的数字没有算进海人。今天的史家也强调：这座岛早在几百年前就是港口。

</More>

<More title={{"en":"How the maps were drawn","zh":"地图是怎么画的"}}>

岛的海岸随年代变化，每个时期的海岸都是从配准过的旧地图上描下来的：城区一带用杰克逊 1822 年的规划图（用到 1822 年）、哥里门 1839 年的地图（1823–1886 年）和 1893 年的城区图（1887–1911 年）；全岛其他地方用英国陆军部 1911–1916 年测的地图（用到 1936 年）、1941 年的地图（1937–1960 年），以及修订到 1954 年的海军海图，西端另用 1960–1961 年的测量（1961 年起）。所以早期城区借用了稍晚的地图，1960 年代裕廊东部的海岸仍是旧图上的样子，海岸线可能偏差几百米。

还有一些缺漏。较小的岛屿，例如绝后岛（今圣淘沙）、乌敏岛、德光岛，没有画出；河流和蓄水池是今天的样子；马来亚的州界取今天的州界，按 1898 年的地图修正过，天定的邦咯诸岛没有画出。在马来亚和区域的大范围视图里，新加坡用的是今天的轮廓。1965 年以后的填海地，任何时期都不出现。

</More>
