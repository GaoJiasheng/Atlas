<Lang en>

On 28 January 1819 ships carrying Stamford Raffles and William Farquhar reached Singapore. Raffles wanted a British East India Company port near the southern entrance to the Straits of Malacca, beyond Dutch control. Trade with China, including tea and opium, made the route valuable. At the river mouth stood the settlement of Temenggong Abdul Rahman. The Company had to negotiate with people who already claimed authority here.

The succession to the Johor-Riau throne was disputed. British officials recognised Hussein, the elder son of the late Sultan Mahmud. His brother Abdul Rahman had already been recognised in Riau by other Malay and Bugis leaders and by the Dutch. Recognising Hussein gave Raffles a ruler with whom to sign an agreement. It did not make the rival claim disappear.

On 6 February 1819 Raffles, Sultan Hussein and the Temenggong signed a treaty permitting the Company to establish a trading post. The agreement promised annual payments. It did not yet transfer the whole island permanently to Britain. Dutch officials protested that Britain had entered territory belonging to the ruler they recognised. British authorities also questioned the risks and legality of keeping the settlement.

Farquhar remained to administer the port. Merchants, boat workers and planters came from nearby settlements as trade grew. His decisions about revenue and the layout of the town later brought him into conflict with Raffles. Their disagreement matters: colonial government was made through contested decisions, and local residents negotiated, worked and lived within their consequences.

<More title={{"en":"The signatories and the rival court","zh":"【待译】The signatories and the rival court"}}>

Hussein and the Temenggong received payments and British recognition, but the treaty also restricted their dealings with other powers. The Riau court and Dutch authorities challenged the agreement. The regalia and the recognition of royal relatives mattered within Malay ideas of legitimate rule; a British signature alone did not settle that question.

NLB's account dates Farquhar's administration from 7 February 1819, while its landing article describes his appointment on 6 February. This draft keeps appointment and beginning administration distinct. Raffles later objected to Farquhar's financial arrangements and occupation of land reserved for government use. Farquhar defended his conduct as necessary to sustain the settlement.

</More>
