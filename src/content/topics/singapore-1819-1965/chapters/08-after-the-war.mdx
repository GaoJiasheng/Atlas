<Lang en>

The British returned to a damaged city in September 1945 and established a military administration. Food shortages, inflation and black-market trading continued after the surrender. Former prisoners, returning families, workers and officials did not experience the transition in the same way. Resistance to Japan had also changed political expectations. Returning to colonial government did not settle who should govern Singapore in the future.

On 1 April 1946 Singapore became a separate Crown Colony. The peninsula's states, together with Penang and Malacca, entered the Malayan Union. Malay opposition to its constitutional terms helped bring about its replacement by the Federation of Malaya in 1948. Singapore remained outside. The British valued its port and bases, while questions about citizenship and the balance of population shaped constitutional proposals on both sides of the strait.

Limited elections began in Singapore in 1948. That June the British declared an Emergency in Malaya after communist guerrillas killed European planters; Singapore followed. The Malayan Communist Party turned to armed struggle, while colonial authorities used detention and security laws against suspected supporters. Residents of resettled villages, union members and families of detainees experienced policies that official military accounts alone cannot describe.

In December 1950 the Maria Hertogh custody case was followed by riots that killed <Num s="S151">18</Num> people and injured <Num s="S151">173</Num>. Constitutional change continued through the Rendel proposals and the 1955 election. David Marshall became Chief Minister. A strike at Hock Lee that May became a riot in which <Num s="S153">four</Num> people died. These deaths belong alongside the political negotiations in an account of the path towards self-government.

The dispute concerned the custody, upbringing and marriage of a girl born to Dutch parents and raised by a Malay Muslim foster mother. Press reporting and public handling of the case intensified religious tensions. Victims included Europeans and Eurasians attacked by participants, and people shot by police. The casualty total should not erase those different circumstances. The riots are remembered because legal decisions and public reporting can put uninvolved people at risk.

</More>

<More title={{"en":"Hock Lee: workers, students and police","zh":"【待译】Hock Lee: workers, students and police"}}>

Workers sought reinstatement and improved conditions; students joined their support. Police said they were restoring access and order. The dead were police officers Andrew Teo Bok Lan and Yuen Yau Pang, journalist Gene Symonds and student Chong Lon Chong. NLB records <Num s="S153">31</Num> injured. Their deaths deserve attention alongside the arguments over responsibility; “workers” and “police” should not replace the people who died.

</More>

<More title={{"en":"What the Emergency meant","zh":"【待译】What the Emergency meant"}}>

British authorities described the Emergency as a campaign against communist violence. Communist accounts stressed anti-colonial struggle. Civilians faced killings, searches, detention, restrictions on movement and resettlement. The existing ww2 source dates the Emergency in Malaya to 18 June 1948 and in Singapore to 24 June; the dates should not be merged.

</More>
