<Lang en>

The town grew around the river, roads and waterfront. Shops, warehouses, lodging houses and homes stood close together. A shophouse combined business at street level with rooms above and behind. Covered walkways sheltered pedestrians, while air wells brought light and ventilation into narrow buildings. These arrangements did not prevent crowded sleeping rooms, poor drainage or the spread of disease. Merchants and labourers could occupy the same street under very different conditions.

Religious and charitable buildings reveal connections across the port. South Bridge Road linked a Hindu temple and a Chulia mosque. Hokkien merchants supported Thian Hock Keng on Telok Ayer Street, then beside the shore. Muslim, Christian and other communities built their own institutions. Tan Tock Seng provided funds for a hospital serving poor people. Schools, burial grounds and clan associations helped newcomers find support beyond the colonial administration.

Mutual help and violence could exist within overlapping networks. A <Term id="kongsi">kongsi</Term> might organise assistance, business or secret-society loyalties; these were not identical organisations. In May 1854 fighting between Hokkien and Teochew groups spread through the town and plantations. Contemporary accounts estimated <Num s="S58">hundreds</Num> killed. Labourers and residents bore losses that lists of merchants and public buildings can leave out.

Reclamation also altered everyday geography. Telok Ayer Street once faced water; later streets and buildings occupied the bay in front of it. Street names preserve selected merchants, officials and places of origin. They are useful clues, but they do not give a complete record of who built the town. Reading the waterfront beside homes, workplaces and burial records makes its social history more visible.
