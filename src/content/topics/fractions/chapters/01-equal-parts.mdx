<Lang en>

You have seen how this lesson works. Now look closely at what happens when you share one thing.

Start with <Beat n={1}>one slice of kaya toast</Beat>. You can see its full outline, with no cuts inside it. This complete slice is one <Term id="whole">whole</Term>. A <Term id="fraction">fraction</Term> names part of a whole, so first you need to know which whole you are sharing.

Look at <Beat n={2}>the three uneven pieces</Beat>. Compare their widths: one piece has more toast than another. Counting three pieces and calling each one a third is a common mistake. Thirds must all be the same amount, so the number of pieces alone cannot tell you that they are thirds.

Now compare <Beat n={3}>the three equal pieces</Beat>. Each strip has the same width and height, so each contains the same amount of toast. These are <Term id="equal-parts">equal parts</Term>. Each piece is one third, or <Frac n={1} d={3} />, of this whole. The two cuts make three parts, including the pieces at both ends.

Next, look at <Beat n={4}>the kueh cut from corner to corner</Beat>. You can imagine turning one triangle over to cover the other exactly. Both pieces contain the same amount of kueh, so each is half of the whole. Equal parts need not have the same shape; what matters is the amount in each part.

Finally, follow the cuts from the centre of <Beat n={5}>the prata in four equal parts</Beat>. Each piece takes up the same amount of the circle, so each is a quarter. All four pieces fit together with no gaps or overlaps. Together they make the whole prata again.

<More title={{ en: "Sharing a kueh lapis", zh: "分千层糕" }}>

At a festive open house, a kueh lapis is cut into slices for the guests. If one slice is thin and another thick, everyone has "one slice", but not the same amount. Sharing fairly means equal parts. That is why a fraction always starts by checking that the parts are equal.

You can also check equal parts by folding a paper strip in half. The two halves cover each other exactly. Each fold in half divides every part into two equal parts, so the number of equal parts doubles.

</More>

<Lang zh>

你已经知道这一课怎么用了。现在仔细看看，把一样东西分开的时候发生了什么。

先看<Beat n={1}>一片 kaya 吐司</Beat>。它只有外面一圈轮廓，里面没有切痕。这完整的一片就是一个<Term id="whole">整体</Term>。<Term id="fraction">分数</Term>说的是一个整体的一部分，所以第一件事是弄清楚：分的是哪一个整体。

再看<Beat n={2}>切得不一样大的 3 块</Beat>。比一比它们的宽度：有一块明显比另一块多。数到 3 块，就说每块是三分之一，这是常见的错误。三分之一的每一份必须一样多，只数块数，看不出它们是不是三分之一。

现在比较<Beat n={3}>平均切成的 3 块</Beat>。每一块一样宽、一样高，所以吐司一样多。这样的部分叫<Term id="equal-parts">平均分</Term>出来的部分。每一块是这个整体的三分之一，写作 <Frac n={1} d={3} />。切 2 刀，得到 3 块，两头的两块也要算上。

接着看<Beat n={4}>沿对角线切开的糕</Beat>。想一想：把一个三角形翻过来，正好能盖住另一个。两块糕一样多，所以每块都是一半。平均分要看的是每份有多少，形状不一定相同。

最后，顺着<Beat n={5}>平均分成 4 份的 prata</Beat>从中心往外看切痕。每一块占圆的一样多，所以每块是四分之一。4 块拼在一起，不留空隙，也不重叠，又是整张 prata。

<More title={{ en: "Sharing a kueh lapis", zh: "分千层糕" }}>

过节去亲友家拜访，主人把千层糕切成片招待客人。如果一片薄、一片厚，大家都拿到"一片"，却不是一样多。公平地分，就是平均分。所以说分数之前，总要先看各部分是不是一样多。

也可以用纸条来检查：把纸条对折，两半正好重合。每对折一次，每一份都被分成相等的两份，所以份数翻一倍。

</More>
