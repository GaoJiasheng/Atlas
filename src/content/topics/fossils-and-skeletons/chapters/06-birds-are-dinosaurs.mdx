The teaching bird represents a Javan myna, a species found in Singapore. Birds belong within <Term id="theropod">theropod</Term> dinosaurs, and theropods belong within dinosaurs. Combining that classification with the bird's local occurrence gives a local example of a living dinosaur. This is an inference joining the classification source and the Singapore species source, rather than a claim taken <Num s="S8,S23">from the myna page alone</Num>.

Look at the wing, then the hindlimbs. The wing is a bird's forelimb. These large body regions provide an observation starting point, but a similar-looking shape alone cannot establish an evolutionary relationship. The model does not turn a Tyrannosaurus into a myna or imply that this bird descended <Num s="S7,S8,S90">directly from that named dinosaur</Num>.

The enlarged feather is a modern-bird comparison piece. Fossils such as Archaeopteryx preserve feathers and a wishbone alongside other features unlike those of living birds. Scientists compare combinations of features when studying early birds. Calling Archaeopteryx an early bird does not identify it as the proven direct <Num s="S10">ancestor of every living bird</Num>.

About <Num s="S11">sixty-six million years ago</Num>, non-avian dinosaurs became extinct; some bird lineages survived. Their surviving branch is <Num s="S8,S11">part of the dinosaur story</Num>. For a nearby observation, watch a bird quietly and describe its visible body regions without chasing or feeding it. That activity supplies observations, not an independent test of ancestry. Dinosaur Earth will follow the wider environment and extinction event on the map.
